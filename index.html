Executive Summary

The new week opens with the same regime but a clearer hierarchy of risks. The immediate market focus is on Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech, yet the deeper issue is no longer simply the next policy-rate move. Long-dated Treasury yields remain elevated, the dollar is near multi-month lows, and gold has pushed to a fresh three-month high. Those moves increasingly point to concern about debt sustainability, policy coordination and the credibility of efforts to manage the long end of the curve.

Energy remains the second major transmission channel. Crude prices eased on Monday as investors awaited details of new U.S. sanctions on Iran, but physical flows through the Strait of Hormuz remain highly constrained and refined-fuel markets in Asia are under severe pressure. The key risk is therefore not just crude price direction on a single day; it is the persistence of supply bottlenecks, elevated refining margins and politically driven trade restrictions that can keep headline inflation sticky even if global growth slows.

A-CLUSTER keeps the regime at Transition / Late-Cycle Stress. There is still no broad credit event and growth is not collapsing, so the evidence does not justify a move to Slowdown. But the balance of market signals remains defensive: high long yields, weak dollar, strong gold and cautious equities suggest investors are assigning a larger premium to fiscal and policy uncertainty. Jackson Hole can alter the near-term narrative; it cannot by itself remove the structural pressure coming from debt, term premium and geopolitics.

Markets are looking to Warsh for clarity on the balance sheet and the Fed's reaction function. With Treasury intervention already affecting the long end, communication about institutional independence now matters almost as much as the rate path.

Hormuz Risk Is Moving From Crude to Refined Fuels

Signal vs. Narrative

Dominant Narrative

“Jackson Hole will determine whether markets turn risk-on or risk-off.”

A-CLUSTER Assessment

Jackson Hole can move expectations at the margin, but the regime is being driven by forces larger than one speech. The decisive variables are the equilibrium level of long yields, fiscal credibility, the path of the dollar, and whether geopolitical supply disruptions continue to keep inflation sticky. Policy communication matters because it changes confidence in those mechanisms, not because it replaces them.

What Would Change Our View?

A return toward Expansion would require long yields to decline on fundamental improvement rather than repeated intervention, a firmer dollar, softer oil and stable credit spreads. A move toward Slowdown would require clearer evidence that tight financial conditions are hitting labor demand, housing, PMIs or corporate earnings. A move toward Contraction would require disorderly credit or liquidity conditions, not simply a correction in equities or crypto.
